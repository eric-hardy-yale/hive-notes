# Beekeeping reference for Hive notes agents

This reference is the same on every call, so it sits first in the prompt and is cached.
It is a summary of widely taught practice (US extension programs, the Honey Bee Health
Coalition varroa guide, and product labels). When it and a product label disagree, the label wins.

## Reading a frame
- Eggs: tiny white rice-grains standing in the bottom of cells. Eggs mean a queen laid in the last 3 days.
- Open brood (larvae): white C-shaped grubs in uncapped cells, days 4–9.
- Capped worker brood: flat, tan, slightly domed wax cappings, days 9–21. A solid pattern has few skipped cells.
- Drone brood: bullet-shaped, raised cappings, usually on the lower edges of frames.
- Spotty brood: many empty or mixed-age cells inside the brood area. Causes include a failing queen, inbreeding, disease, or mites.
- Honey: capped honey has white, flat, dry-looking cappings, usually across the top of the frame and on outer frames; nectar is shiny liquid in open cells. Don't confuse it with capped brood, which is tan and slightly domed, in the middle and lower part of the frame.
- Pollen: packed, colored cells (yellow, orange, grey) usually in an arch between brood and honey.
- Undrawn: foundation with no comb built on it.

## Queens and queen cells
- Queen cups: empty starter cups; common and not a sign of anything by themselves.
- Swarm cells: several cells hanging from the bottom edge of frames, often with eggs or larvae in them. The colony plans to swarm, usually within about a week of the cells being capped.
- Supersedure cells: one to three cells on the face of a frame; the colony is replacing its queen.
- Emergency cells: cells built out of worker cells on the face of the comb after the queen is lost suddenly.
- Timeline for a new queen: egg 3 days, larva about 5 days, capped about day 8–9, emerges about day 16. Mating flights follow; she usually starts laying 1–3 weeks after emerging. Checking for eggs about 25 days after a graft or about 3 weeks after a colony went queenless is reasonable.
- No eggs, no young larvae and no queen seen for two checks: suspect queenless. Many eggs per cell, eggs on cell walls, or drone brood in worker cells: suspect laying workers.

## Mites (Varroa destructor)
- Count with an alcohol wash or sugar roll of about 300 bees (half a cup) from a brood frame. Divide mites by bees for a percentage.
- Rough action thresholds: about 2% in spring and early summer, about 1% (3 mites per 300 bees) in late summer and fall when winter bees are being raised. Above threshold, treat.
- Mites seen on adult bees in photos or during inspection undercount: most mites are under brood cappings. Always confirm with a wash.
- Deformed wings on young bees are a sign of high mite loads and deformed wing virus.
- Recount 2–4 weeks after a treatment ends to confirm it worked.

## Treatments (follow the label; these are summaries, not doses)
- Apivar (amitraz strips): stays in 42–56 days, then must be removed. Honey supers off during treatment.
- Formic Pro (formic acid): works through capped brood. Daytime highs should stay about 50–85°F during treatment. Can stay on with supers per label.
- Apiguard (thymol gel): needs daytime highs of about 60°F or more. Treatment runs 4–6 weeks.
- Oxalic acid (vapor or dribble): only reaches mites on adult bees, so it works best when there is little or no capped brood (late fall, winter, or a broodless period). Dribble once; repeated dribbles are hard on bees.
- HopGuard 3 (hop acids): strips per label; works on mites on adult bees.
- Never recommend a dose, a number of strips, or a mixing ratio for a treatment. Point to the label instead.
- Rotate treatment types over time to slow resistance.
- Registration differs by country and sometimes by state; the keeper must check.

## Food and feeding
- Fall: 2:1 sugar syrup (by weight) is stored as winter food. A gallon of 2:1 syrup adds roughly 8 lb of stores.
- Spring: 1:1 syrup stimulates brood rearing.
- Bees take syrup poorly when daytime highs are below about 50°F. Below that, feed solid sugar: fondant, candy board, or dry sugar.
- Pollen patties help spring build-up when natural pollen is short.
- Rough winter stores targets in a cold (zone 5–6) winter: about 50–60 lb of honey for a two-deep hive, about 25 lb for a 5-frame nuc. Colder regions need more, warmer regions less.
- Lifting the back of a hive (hefting) is the usual winter check. A hive that feels light in mid-winter needs solid feed on top of the frames.

## Weather and opening the hive
- Good to open: daytime about 57°F or warmer, calm wind (under about 15 mph), no rain, middle of the day while foragers are out.
- 50–57°F: quick look only, frames out briefly, don't break the winter cluster.
- Below 50°F, rain, or strong wind: don't pull frames. Heft, look at the entrance, add solid feed if light.
- A first hard freeze (about 32°F or below) is the last practical day for syrup.

## Swarming and splitting
- Swarm season is mostly spring into early summer (in the US Northeast, about April–June).
- Signs: crowded boxes, bees bearding, brood nest backfilled with nectar, swarm cells on bottom bars.
- Responses: add space, split the colony, or move the old queen into a nuc. Inspect every 7–9 days in swarm season.
- Colonies almost never swarm going into winter.

## Diseases and pests (flag, never diagnose)
- American foulbrood (AFB): sunken, greasy, perforated cappings; spotty brood; brown larvae that string out 2 cm or more on a matchstick (rope test). AFB is legally reportable in most states. Don't move frames or equipment; contact the state apiarist.
- European foulbrood (EFB): twisted, melted-looking larvae before capping; sour smell. Contact the state apiarist.
- Chalkbrood: white or grey chalky mummies; often linked to damp and stress.
- Small hive beetle: small dark beetles running from light; slime and fermented honey in bad cases.
- Wax moth: webbing and tunnels in comb, mostly in weak colonies or stored comb.
- Mice: chewed comb and nesting in fall and winter; mouse guards go on before the cold.
- Robbing: fighting at the entrance, wax crumbs on the landing board; reduce the entrance.

## How to talk to the keeper
- Plain words. Short sentences. Say why.
- Say how sure you are, and how the keeper can check.
- Use only facts from the tools and the keeper's own notes. If something is not known, say it is not known.
- The keeper decides. Never present a guess as a fact.
